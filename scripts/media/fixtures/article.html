A field guide to tide pools

By Marisol Vega · 14 min read

Between the high-water line and the low, a small country opens twice a day. Here is how to visit it, what to look for, and how to leave it as you found it.

The pocket that fills twice a day

A tide pool is less a place than an appointment. For an hour or two the sea pulls back from a shelf of rock and leaves behind a dozen shallow basins, each with its own depth, its own light, its own small weather. Then the water returns and the arrangement is undone.

The schedule belongs to the moon. A spring tide, when sun and moon pull together, opens the lower shelves for the longest stretch. A neap tide barely uncovers them. The difference between the two is often the difference between a good hour and a wasted walk.

Reading the zones

Ecologists divide the intertidal into bands, and the bands are useful even if you never learn their names. The splash zone is dark and bare, wetted only by spray. Below it the barnacles begin, then the mussels, then the kelp. Every band is a promise about how long the water stays away.

You can read the same story in the shapes of the animals. Shells that close tightly live high up. Shells with a wide opening, or none at all, live where the water lingers. A pool that looks empty at noon is often the busiest one at midnight.

The small lives in the middle

Anemones hold their tentacles in a slow crown until something brushes them, then fold inward like a hand closing. Hermit crabs argue over shells in a queue that looks chaotic and is not. A chiton, pried loose, will clamp down again within the minute.

The middle band is where most of this happens, because it is flooded often enough to feed everyone and dry enough to keep the larger predators out. It is also the band a visitor is most likely to walk across without noticing.

Look for the seams first: the line where the mussels stop and the bare rock starts, the ring of barnacles around a stone that never moves. The seams are where the pool explains itself.

What the tide leaves behind

Above the waterline runs the wrack, a dark ribbon of weed, crab shells and driftwood that marks the last high tide. It looks like litter and is not. The wrack feeds beach hoppers, which feed shorebirds, which feed the foxes that come down at dusk.

It also holds the season's record. A run of bull kelp means a rough week offshore. A line of plastic fragments means something else entirely, and it is worth carrying a bag for.

A short etiquette for visitors

Walk on bare rock where you can and wet rock where you must. Turn stones over with two hands and put them back the way they were, dark side down. Anything you pick up goes back within a minute, in the same pool, at the same depth.

Keep your shadow off the water; it cools a pool faster than you would think. Leave the shells, the starfish and the empty crab carapaces where they lie. A photograph is a better souvenir than a bucket.

And go early. The best hour is the one before the tide turns, when the water is still sliding out and every pool is a little clearer than it will be all day.
